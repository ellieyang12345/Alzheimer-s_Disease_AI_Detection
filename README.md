# Alzheimer's Disease AI Detection
Why does this matter? Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia and is estimated to affect around 55 million people around the world. It is one of the leading causes of death, characterized by progressive cognitive deterioration, and is exhibited through behavioral changes and a decline in daily activity. The estimate of its prevalence is one in nine people aged 65 years and older, with 6.9 million people diagnosed in the United States, and almost three times higher for people aged 85 and older. AD cost $226 billion dollars in 2016 alone, and, within the United states, consistently worths more than $100 billion dollars to treat in a primary care setting, serving as one of the most costly diseases in the world.
AD is caused by the accumulation of amyloid beta plaques and tau tangles, ultimately depleting brain health. It harms nerve cells in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, areas responsible for memory, later affecting the cerebral cortex, an area in charge of language, reasoning, and social behavior. Amyloid precursor protein is prevalent in the brain and helps maintain brain health, cellular health, and communication between cells through the formation of synapses. Amyloid precursor protein is cleaved into smaller subunits, one of which is amyloid beta which the brain removes regarding it as waste. In AD, these amyloid beta aggregate together, resulting in the hindering of cell to cell communication between neurons in the brain. The tau protein serves as structural support for nerve cells and, in a healthy brain, undergoes phosphorylation. However, in AD, they experience overphosphorylation and consequently form tangles in the brain, blocking neuron transport systems, as a result, leading to cell death. In the presence of AD, the brain experiences a perpetual vicious cycle where the release of inflammatory molecules causes more tangles of tau protein and aggregation of amyloid beta plaques and ultimately more cell death. In addition, the overactivation of the glial cells in order to combat the disease also harms the nerve cells. AD results in the loss of memory, language, cognition, and problem-solving skills. It's primarily manifested as memory loss and executive dysfunction, leading to a change in behavior, vision, language, and ultimately death.

This project seeks to detect AD via different gene pathways (different from the normal analysis of amyloid beta and tau tangles that onset AD). It's an AI tool that has been tested by public dataset. 

# Methodology
How did I do it? 
    To acquire the gene expression profiles, I accessed publicly available Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets. Through a comparative study between the Mild Cognitive Impairment(MCI) and the Control groups, the top ~250 Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs) related to the early onset of Alzheimer's Disease were obtained.
     To further prioritize the results based on the most significant biological correlation, the top 100 genes were filtered, then inspected using the STRING database. Through this approach, Protein-Protein Interactions (PPI) networks for the significantly associated top 100 genes were created based on high-confidence, 0.900, functional/physical associations, rather than co-expression correlation alone.
     The constructed PPI network was then imported to Cytoscape for topological analyses. Specifically, the study concentrated on the "Hub" network nodes that are highly interconnected, as well as the "Modules" that are most strongly connected, providing a high level of guaranteed biological associations, ensuring that the selected genes are not isolated, but represent a well-integrated molecular neighborhood related to the disease.
     Finally, the Functional Enrichment Analysis, using the Reactome Pathway Database, pinpointed the corresponding significant *mitochondrial* associated pathways that have been disturbed with very low q-values: $FDR < 3.66
     The discovered pathways are then the "indicators" for the AI-based "detection system".
